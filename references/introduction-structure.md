# Introduction Structure — the Funnel

Read this before drafting or revising any Introduction section. The Introduction is where most hydrology papers fail before reviewers finish the second page. The fix is structural, not stylistic.

## The funnel (also called the hourglass-top)

A well-built Introduction narrows from broad to specific in five distinct moves. Each move corresponds to one or two paragraphs. Skip a move and the section feels disconnected. Compress two moves into one paragraph and the reader loses the chain.

### Move 1 — Broad significance (1 paragraph)

Open with why the broader topic matters. Not the specific study system. Not the specific method. The category of problem.

For a hydrology paper this is usually: water resources under stress, groundwater dependence, climate impact on hydrologic systems, the management or policy stakes of the kind of system you study.

The opening sentence sets the entire tone. Make it concrete, not generic. "Groundwater supplies 50% of irrigation water in semi-arid agricultural regions" is concrete. "Groundwater is an important resource" is generic and reviewers groan.

End this paragraph with a transition that signals you are about to narrow toward your specific scientific problem.

### Move 2 — Narrowing to the scientific problem (1–2 paragraphs)

Move from "this category matters" to "the open scientific question within this category". This is where the literature review starts, but it is review with a purpose, not an inventory.

Cite to support claims about the state of knowledge, not to demonstrate awareness of every paper in the area. Group prior work by approach or finding, not chronologically. "Three approaches have been used to model `<X>`: `<approach 1>` (Author1, Year; Author2, Year), `<approach 2>` (Author3, Year), and `<approach 3>` (Author4, Year). Each has tradeoffs." That is purposeful review.

### Move 3 — The specific gap (1 paragraph, often the shortest)

State explicitly what is unknown, untested, or unresolved. The gap should be a single sentence that a reviewer can underline. "Despite extensive use of process-based models in `<study setting>`, no published study has tested whether `<proposed approach>` outperforms `<established alternatives>` across a dense observation network."

The gap must follow logically from Move 2. If a reviewer cannot trace the gap back to the specific prior work cited, the funnel is broken.

Common gap-statement failures:
- Naming a gap that prior work has actually addressed (the agent's literature search was incomplete)
- Naming a gap so narrow it does not justify a full paper ("no one has tested this on `<study area>` specifically" is not a gap; it is a study-area statement)
- Stating the gap as the absence of your own method ("no one has used `<your method>` for this problem" is method-driven, not problem-driven)

### Move 4 — Study objectives and hypothesis (1 paragraph)

State what the present study does about the gap. Use numbered or explicit objectives:

> "This study addresses this gap by (i) developing a `<class of model>` with explicit `<process A>` and `<process B>` terms, (ii) calibrating the model across `<N>` stations in `<study region>`, and (iii) evaluating performance separately for `<subgroup 1>` and `<subgroup 2>`."

A formal hypothesis is optional in hydrology (more common in experimental fields), but a clearly stated expected finding strengthens the framing: "It is hypothesised that explicit `<process A>` terms will improve fit at `<subgroup 1>` stations but not `<subgroup 2>`."

### Move 5 — Roadmap (1 short paragraph or final sentence)

Tell the reader what comes next. One or two sentences. "Section 2 describes the study area, data, and `<model class>` formulation. Section 3 reports calibration results across `<subgroup 1>` and `<subgroup 2>` subsets. Section 4 interprets the fitted parameters physically and discusses transferability."

Some journals discourage the roadmap as filler. JHRS tolerates it; HJ readers expect it for longer papers. If the paper is short and the structure is conventional IMRAD, the roadmap can be cut.

## Length budget

A typical Introduction in HJ or JHRS runs 800–1,200 words across 4–6 paragraphs. Shorter than 600 words usually means the literature review is thin. Longer than 1,500 words usually means Move 2 has expanded into a review article — cut it back.

## Verb tense in the Introduction

- **Established facts and current knowledge:** present tense ("groundwater supplies", "tidal loading affects")
- **Specific past studies and their findings:** past tense ("Author (Year) showed", "Authors (Year) found")
- **Statement of objectives for the present study:** present tense ("This study evaluates", not "This study will evaluate" — future tense reads as a proposal, not a paper)

## Self-check before finalizing the Introduction

1. Can a reviewer point to the single sentence that states the gap?
2. Does the gap statement follow logically from cited prior work, or is it asserted?
3. Are the study objectives a direct response to the gap, or do they introduce new scope not motivated by the funnel?
4. Is the first sentence concrete, not generic?
5. Is the literature review purposeful (grouped by approach/finding) or inventory-style (chronological list)?
6. Is the broad-to-specific narrowing visible across the paragraphs, or do paragraphs mix scales?
7. If you removed any cited paper, would a claim become unsupported? If no, the citation is decorative — cut it.

If any answer is no, restructure before presenting.

## What a broken Introduction looks like

The most common failure pattern: the Introduction reads as a list of related papers with one paragraph per topic, ending with "however, more research is needed in this area" and a vague objective. No funnel. No specific gap. No logical chain.

If your draft fits that pattern, do not just rewrite the conclusion. The whole section needs restructuring around the five moves.
